The problem with conventional insulation

Solid-wall buildings constructed before about 1919 were built to a different moisture model. Lime mortar, solid masonry and permeable finishes allow the wall to absorb moisture and release it again. There is no cavity, no membrane and no designed drainage plane; the drying capacity of the assembly is the moisture strategy.

Introducing a vapour-resistant insulation layer on the internal face changes two things at once. It lowers the temperature of the masonry behind the insulation, which increases the relative humidity within the wall, and it removes the drying path that previously ran inwards. The result is accumulation at the insulation–masonry interface.

The consequences are well documented and familiar to anyone who has surveyed a failed retrofit:

  • Interstitial condensation at the cold face of the insulation
  • Elevated moisture content in embedded timbers, particularly joist ends built into the wall
  • Frost damage and spalling where masonry cannot dry before a freeze cycle
  • Salt migration and efflorescence at the interface
  • Mould growth at thermal bridges, typically reveals and floor junctions
  • Degraded thermal performance, since wet masonry conducts heat substantially faster than dry

Why the system exists

BreatheTherm was developed by two builders rather than a materials company. Josh Tetley founded HF Limited in 2003; Greg Podolak joined in 2007, and together they launched HF Building in 2016 specialising in conservation and retrofit of period properties.

Across two decades of that work the same pattern recurred: products specified in good faith were damaging the buildings they were installed to improve. The system was developed to combine the vapour-open behaviour of traditional lime construction with thermal performance closer to that of modern insulation, rather than asking a specifier to choose between the two.

How the system works

The wall assembly

Applied directly to prepared masonry. No frame, no battens, no service void, no membrane. The absence of a cavity behind the insulation is deliberate — an air gap at the cold face of an insulation layer is where interstitial condensation collects.

  1. Existing substrate — solid brick, stone, cob, flint or timber-frame infill, prepared back to sound material
  2. PerliScratch — lime-perlite scratch coat providing a keyed, high-adhesion base
  3. PerliTherm — expanded perlite bound with hydraulic lime; the primary insulating and moisture-buffering layer
  4. AeroBond + mesh — fibre-reinforced lime-polymer adhesive and basecoat with reinforcement, replacing separate primer, adhesive and basecoat
  5. Aerogel layer — optional, used selectively where build depth is constrained
  6. PerliFinish — breathable lime finish coat

How moisture moves through the assembly

Three mechanisms operate together. Vapour diffusion moves moisture through the assembly along the vapour pressure gradient; because every layer has a low Sd value, there is no point at which that flow is arrested. Capillary transport moves liquid water back towards the drying face through the pore structure of the lime-perlite matrix, which is the mechanism conventional insulation lacks entirely. Hygroscopic buffering absorbs moisture at humidity peaks and releases it as conditions dry, damping the spikes that produce surface condensation.

Why perlite, and why lime

Perlite is a naturally occurring volcanic mineral. Heated, it expands and traps air, producing a lightweight aggregate that is insulating, non-combustible and open-pored. Bound in hydraulic lime it produces a render that is softer than the masonry it is applied to, vapour-open, and capillary active — three properties a cement- or gypsum-based product cannot offer simultaneously.

Lime matters for compatibility as much as for breathability. A binder harder than the substrate concentrates stress at the interface and causes the render to fail before the wall does. On historic masonry the sacrificial layer should always be the finish, not the fabric.

Why aerogel

Silica aerogel has the lowest thermal conductivity of any usable solid insulant at 0.017 W/m·K, and it remains vapour-open. Its role in the system is specific rather than general: it is used where build depth is critically constrained and perlite alone cannot reach the target U-value — window reveals, recessed frames, tight junctions and rooms where losing 50 mm from the wall face would alter the proportions unacceptably. It costs roughly five times as much per millimetre of build-up saved, so specifying it across whole elevations is rarely justified.

Technical FAQs

How does BreatheTherm compare to wood fibre systems?
Both are vapour-open and capillary active, so both are legitimate choices on a solid wall. The differences are that BreatheTherm is mineral rather than organic, is applied wet so it follows irregular substrates without a frame or void, and reaches a comparable U-value in less depth. Wood fibre is generally faster to install on a flat, regular substrate and has lower embodied carbon.
How does it compare to PIR?
PIR has a better lambda value, at roughly 0.022 against 0.064 for perlite, so it reaches a given U-value in less depth. It is also effectively vapour-tight, which on a solid wall removes the assembly's inward drying path. On a cavity-walled modern building that is not a problem. On solid masonry it is the mechanism by which internal insulation fails.
What happens if moisture enters the wall?
It moves through and dries out, which is the design intent rather than a failure mode. The lime-perlite matrix buffers liquid and vapour and returns moisture towards the drying face by capillary action. What the system cannot do is compensate for an unresolved defect — a leaking gutter, failed pointing or raised external ground level must be fixed before insulating.
Internal or external application?
The system as documented here is an internal application, which is what heritage and conservation contexts generally require. For external application on unlisted buildings, contact the technical team to discuss substrate, exposure and finish.
What thickness is typically required?
50 mm of PerliTherm plus scratch and finish coats is the standard wall build-up, giving a total added depth of around 75 mm. Where that is unacceptable, 25 mm has been used successfully on completed projects, and a 10 mm aerogel layer can be introduced to recover performance in constrained areas.
Can it be used with lime render or lime plaster?
Yes — the system is lime-based throughout and PerliFinish is itself a lime finish coat. It is compatible with existing lime plaster and with traditional lime render externally. Gypsum plaster and cement render should not be used over it.
Is hygrothermal analysis available?
Yes. A site-specific assessment is produced for every project, and modelling output can be issued to support a Listed Building Consent application or a client's technical review.
Can it be specified on a listed building?
Yes, subject to Listed Building Consent, which is required for internal works affecting character regardless of the system used. The system has been used in Grade I, Grade II* and Grade II listed buildings. More on consent.
