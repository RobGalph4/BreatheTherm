Internal wall insulation for listed and period buildings

How to insulate a solid-wall, listed or conservation-area property without trapping moisture in the fabric — consent, build-ups, performance and evidence.

Roughly 5.9 million buildings in the UK were built before 1919. Almost all of them have solid walls, almost all of them are cold, and almost all of the insulation sold in this country is designed for a completely different kind of building.

This page covers what internal wall insulation involves in a listed or period property, what consent you need, what performance is realistically achievable, and how a vapour-open system differs from the boards most contractors will offer you.

Why internal rather than external

External wall insulation is usually cheaper per square metre and avoids disruption inside the house. For a listed building or a property in a conservation area it is almost always the wrong answer, because it changes the external appearance. Brick bond, stone coursing, string courses, window reveals and eaves detail are the things that give a period building its character, and they are exactly the things external insulation buries.

Internal wall insulation keeps the outside of the building untouched. That single fact removes most of the planning objections, and it is why almost every heritage retrofit is done internally.

The trade-off is that internal insulation makes the wall itself colder. The masonry now sits outside the insulation layer rather than inside it, so it stays wetter for longer after rain and it loses the small drying benefit it used to get from indoor heat. A vapour-closed system in that situation traps moisture at the interface between insulation and wall. A vapour-open system lets it keep moving. That difference is the whole argument.

What makes a system suitable for a historic wall

Vapour permeability

Moisture must be able to pass through the assembly rather than accumulate at a boundary. In practice this means low Sd values through every layer, with no polythene, foil facing or impermeable membrane anywhere in the build-up.

Hygroscopic buffering

Lime and perlite absorb moisture at humidity peaks and release it again as conditions dry. That buffering smooths out the spikes that cause surface condensation and mould.

Material compatibility

Lime-based, mineral, and softer than the masonry it is applied to. Cement renders and gypsum plasters are harder than historic brick and stone, and they fail at the interface.

Thin build-up

Every millimetre taken off a window reveal changes the proportions of the room. A system that performs at 25–50 mm preserves detail that a 100 mm board destroys.

Continuity at junctions

Most failures happen where the insulation stops: reveals, floor junctions, joist ends and party walls. The system has to have an answer for each of them.

Documented evidence

A conservation officer needs something to point at. WTA Merkblatt 6-4 is the internationally recognised standard for internal insulation of historic buildings.

Do you need planning permission or consent?

Three different regimes get confused with each other constantly. They are not the same thing.

Situation What is required
Unlisted house, not in a conservation area No planning permission for internal work. Building Regulations still apply where the work is a controlled thermal element.
Unlisted house in a conservation area Conservation area controls govern external appearance. Internal insulation does not usually engage them.
Listed building, any grade Listed Building Consent is required for works affecting character, and internal insulation almost always does. Applies to the interior as well as the exterior.

Carrying out works to a listed building without consent is a criminal offence, not a planning technicality. It is worth saying plainly because homeowners are frequently told otherwise.

What helps a consent application succeed is evidence that the proposal will not harm the fabric. BreatheTherm supplies hygrothermal modelling reports, material data and a record of comparable approvals to support applications. Your conservation officer will usually want to see the proposed build-up, the vapour resistance of each layer, and how reveals and joist ends are detailed.

The build-up

A typical internal wall specification uses four or five layers applied directly to the prepared masonry. There is no frame, no battens and no cavity behind the insulation, which is deliberate: a void behind an insulation layer is where moisture collects.

What performance can you expect?

Achievable U-values depend on the existing wall and the build-up depth available. Across the range of specifications the system delivers between 0.17 and 0.45 W/m²K.

0.35 W/m²K from 50 mm PerliTherm on a 215 mm solid brick wall
0.17 W/m²K combining PerliTherm with a 10 mm aerogel layer
65% Average reduction in heat loss across completed projects

A site-specific hygrothermal calculation is produced for every project rather than a generic figure, because the same build-up on a sheltered south wall and an exposed west gable behaves differently. Full technical specification and modelling detail.

What happens next

  1. Free property assessment. A specialist visits, inspects the wall construction, checks for existing moisture problems and measures what depth is available.
  2. Written recommendation. A proposed build-up, the U-value it achieves, an indicative cost, and a hygrothermal risk note.
  3. Consent support, if needed. Documentation for a Listed Building Consent application.
  4. Installation. Typically 2–5 days per room, or 1–3 weeks for a whole house. Lime plaster then needs 3–7 days to cure before decoration.
